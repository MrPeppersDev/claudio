Rapid serial visual presentation, commonly abbreviated RSVP, is a reading technique in which words appear sequentially at a fixed location on the screen, eliminating the eye movements that ordinary reading requires. Each word is shown briefly, replaced by the next, and the reader's gaze remains stationary throughout the presentation. Proponents argue that this method increases reading speed because the time normally spent on saccades and fixation transitions is recovered as additional comprehension time. Critics counter that the absence of regression — the ability to look back when a sentence is misparsed — undermines comprehension on complex material, since readers cannot recover from interpretive errors that they would ordinarily repair through a quick backward glance.

The technique has roots in mid-twentieth-century reading research, but it gained popular attention through commercial applications such as Spritz, Reedy, and Voice Dream. More recent academic studies have explored variations: variable word duration scaled to word length, sentence-final pauses to support integration, and combinations of RSVP with traditional reading layouts that preserve some context. The trade-off between raw words-per-minute speed and sustained comprehension remains a contested empirical question, particularly for technical or argumentative texts where readers benefit measurably from the option to backtrack.
